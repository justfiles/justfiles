One desktop.
Now a household.

The shell was designed for two actors — you and Frieren — sharing one workspace. Everything downstream assumed it: one drawer, one face, one agents/main/. Five ways to open that up to a party, without turning your computer into a staffing tool. Every mockup below is live — press the faces.

before the directions

Six questions any answer has to survive

These are the pressures that break a one-agent UI the moment there are four. Each direction below is scored against them, not against how it looks.

  1. 01Who am I talking to?Never ambiguous, never a guess — one glance, no reading.
  2. 02Who is working right now?And on what, without opening anything.
  3. 03Who handed what to whom?Delegation is the whole point and the easiest thing to hide.
  4. 04Who needs me?An agent blocked on a decision has to be able to interrupt — quietly.
  5. 05Where do they talk together?A room with several of them in it, that you can also speak into.
  6. 06How do I make one?The summoning ritual, repeated — and cheap enough to repeat.
01

edge-anchored · smallest change

The Rail

The drawer grows a spine. A column of physical keycaps is welded to the right edge — one per agent, faces out. Press a cap and it stays down: the drawer slides out from behind it, and the depressed cap is the only "who am I talking to" indicator the screen needs. Press it again and it pops; the drawer goes with it.

How it works

The rail is the drawer's outer wall, always visible at zero cost. Caps use the shell's existing keycap recipe — 1.5px edge, press eats the edge — so "which agent" is stated by lighting and depth rather than a highlight colour. Working agents breathe a green ring; blocked ones wear a gold badge. A welded double-cap is a group thread.

What it costs

Small.

You already have the drawer, the slide, the resizer and MessagesPanel. This adds a rail component, a per-agent session key, and an agent picker. Nothing in the window model moves.

Where it's weak

  • One conversation at a time — the party thread is the only place they meet.
  • Above ~8 agents the rail needs scrolling, and it stops reading as a set of people.
  • Costs a permanent ~64px of screen edge, which the omnibar work deliberately reclaimed.

Reads as

A mixing desk. Channel strips with faces on them. Tactile and completely unambiguous — the strongest answer to "who am I talking to" of the five, and the weakest to "who handed what to whom".

02

a window · scales furthest

The House

Kill the drawer. The household gets a real window — roster, threads, conversation — that drags, tiles and fullscreens like everything else. Group threads are first-class rows, not a mode. Delegation renders inline in the transcript, so a handoff you weren't watching is still legible an hour later.

How it works

Three columns: presence rail (status by ring — green working, gold needs-you), thread list with last lines, conversation. Group threads carry a face-stack instead of one face. A handoff writes a chip into the transcript naming both parties and the job, so the record survives without you having watched it.

What it costs

Medium.

A new app-shaped window plus a thread model. The drawer and its resizer go away, which is a deletion, not a migration. Fits the existing OsWindow layer with no changes to it.

Where it's weak

  • Your household becomes an app you visit. Co-presence — the thing that made the shell feel inhabited — is gone.
  • It is the most familiar answer, which means it is also the least yours. This is Slack with nicer avatars.
  • An agent that needs you must interrupt from outside the window, so you still need a second surface for that.

Reads as

A team you employ. Correct for four coworkers; slightly wrong for a family. Scales to twenty agents better than anything else here and will never surprise anyone.

03

no panel at all · the brave one

The Inhabitants

No rail, no window, no drawer. Your agents live on the desktop. They stand where they are working — Fern on the Files window she is filing into, Stark down at the corner with a progress ring — and they speak in bubbles anchored to themselves. Delegation is visible motion: one walks to another and a thread is drawn between them.

How it works

Agents are desktop residents, like Stickies but alive. Position is status: on a window means working in it, dimmed in a corner means idle, a dashed tether means two are paired on one job. Everyone shows one short line at rest; clicking expands that agent into a composer in place. Drag one onto another to put them on the same job.

What it costs

High.

A new spatial layer that has to negotiate with the window manager (occlusion, drag, focus), a placement engine, and a real accessibility story — sprites on a wallpaper are not a list. Long transcripts need a "pull up the scrollback" gesture that doesn't exist yet.

Where it's weak

  • Reading a 40-message history in a speech bubble is bad. This needs one of the other directions underneath it for depth.
  • Six agents on a wallpaper is charming; twelve is an infestation.
  • Windows and inhabitants will fight for the same pixels forever.

Reads as

A house with people in it. This is the only direction that answers family and friends as naturally as it answers team, and the only one no one else has. It is also the one most likely to be loved and least likely to survive contact with a 40-message transcript.

04

no new surface · keyboard-first

The Bench

Add nothing. The omnibar already is the one intent surface — so the household sits on it. A bench of faces on the pill's shoulder; typing routes three ways now (an app, whoever's listening, or @fern); and the sheet above becomes a braided transcript where every line wears its speaker's face.

How it works

The bench is five faces on the pill's left shoulder. The lifted, coloured one is who the composer is addressed to — the same fact the placeholder states in words. Typing @ filters the bench into a menu; nothing is hidden behind a shortcut you have to be told about. The sheet's braid keeps everyone in one scroll, so a handoff is read where it happened.

What it costs

Low.

The pill, the sheet, the transcript and the routing already exist. This adds a bench, an @ route in the existing matcher, and a speaker on each transcript row. It removes a surface rather than adding one — the only direction here that makes the shell simpler.

Where it's weak

  • The sheet is transient by design. A household that lives in a surface that closes when you press Escape will feel provisional.
  • Past six or seven the bench is a row of thumbnails, not faces.
  • No per-agent history view, so "what did Eisen say last Tuesday" has nowhere to go.

Reads as

One door for everything, exactly as the omnibar doc argued. The fastest to use and the least ceremonial — which cuts against a product whose personalization flow is a summoning ritual.

05

a zoomed-out view · composes with 1, 2 or 4

The Table

Not a chat surface — the view above one. Press a key and the desktop recedes into a table with your household seated round it, the jobs as cards on the wood, and dashed threads showing who passed what to whom. Drag a card onto a seat to delegate. This is the answer to the question chat is worst at: what is everyone doing?

How it works

A Mission-Control-style takeover, summoned and dismissed. Seats show state by ring; cards are live jobs; dashed threads are the handoffs that already happened. The centre is shared memory — the thing that makes them one household rather than five chatbots — and the side panel answers "what is this one actually doing" in four lines, no scrolling.

What it costs

High — but additive.

It needs a job model the shell doesn't have yet: tasks as first-class objects with an owner and a provenance trail. That's the real work, and it's worth doing regardless. The table is then a view over it.

Where it's weak

  • You cannot talk here, so it is never the whole answer — it needs 1, 2 or 4 underneath it.
  • With two agents and no parallel work it is an empty ceremony.
  • Round-table geometry stops being readable past about eight seats.

Reads as

The party's camp, or a war room, depending on your cast. The only direction that makes parallel work feel like the point rather than a thing you discover by scrolling.

if you want my read

Ship one, prototype one, hold one

They are not five candidates for the same slot. Two of them compose, one is a trap, and one is the reason to do this at all.

ship

01 · The Rail

Your instinct was right and it's the cheapest true multi-agent change available. It reuses the drawer, states "who am I talking to" in the shell's own physical language, and buys time to learn what people actually do with four agents before you build something expensive.

prototype

03 · The Inhabitants

The only one that is unmistakably this product. Build it as a layer over the Rail's session model, not instead of it — sprites for presence and quick exchanges, the drawer still there for depth. If the ambient layer works, the rail can quietly shrink.

hold

05 · The Table

Blocked on a job model that doesn't exist yet. Build the jobs — owner, provenance, state — because 01 and 03 both want them anyway. The table then costs a view rather than a system.

the trap

02 · The House

It scores best on the matrix and it is the wrong answer. It is Slack, it will work, and it turns a desktop you live in with someone into an app where you manage staff. Take its handoff chips and its group threads; leave the window.

Mockups are stylized — UI is drawn oversized for reading at this scale. Cast, transcripts and timings are invented. Paths (/system/agents/<id>/, /system/memory/daily/) and the keycap press are the real ones.
