platform/frieren · direction 01 · the rail

The right edge,
in detail.

Your three questions first — two of them already have answers written into app.css, and one of my mockups got a fact wrong. Then four ways to build the rail on top of those answers.

question one

Does the whole desktop move, menubar and all?

Today: yes, and my mockup drew it wrong. .frn-shell is a flex row; .frn-desktop-frame holds the menubar and the desktop and is the flex:1 sibling that squishes; .frn-chat-panel is flex:0 0 auto with an animated width. So the menubar travels. I drew it spanning the full width, which is the other option — worth comparing properly.

verdict · keep the push, promote the rail

Push wins, and the rail becomes a third slab that never travels. The shell goes from two children to three: frame · drawer · rail, with the rail outside .frn-desktop-frame so it sits in the same place whether the drawer is out or shut. That is the whole point of a physical button.

Four reasons the overlay loses. One: it re-opens a decision the omnibar doc already closed — “a surface is either collapsed or unambiguously on top; nothing in between.” A resident drawer lying over the desktop is exactly that middle state. Two: co-presence needs both actors visible. If the drawer covers the window you are talking about, you cannot watch the agent act on it. Three: the seam is made of inset shadows describing a cut. Float the drawer and you get a drop shadow instead — a sheet above, not a drawer beneath — and the material has to be thrown away. Four: Frieren's menubar is not macOS's. It is captioned by the focused app (focusedApp?.name ?? 'Just Computer'), so it is the desktop region's caption, not the machine's. A caption should narrow with the thing it captions.

One real exception: fullscreen. The menubar is already hidden there (fullscreenId ? null : <Menubar>) and squeezing a fullscreen app is a contradiction — so in fullscreen the drawer should overlay, at the z-index:9001 the panel already carries for exactly this. Two behaviours, one rule: the drawer pushes what can be pushed and covers what cannot.

What this changes in code

  • clampChatWidth() must subtract the rail: innerWidth − MIN_DESKTOP_WIDTH − RAIL_W.
  • The rail moves out of .frn-desktop-frame into .frn-shell.
  • The rail must render when fullscreenId is set — unlike the dock, which hides.

What stays exactly as it is

The 460ms cubic-bezier(.32,.72,0,1) travel, the width-as-travel trick, the resizer at the seam, and the .frn-chat-body lag. None of that is touched by adding a rail.

question two

Is the dock still app icons, or just a search bar?

Still icons. At rest the bar is 62px tall and holds four things, in this order: a 132px search slot, a row of 44px app icons with running dots, a hairline separator, and the 46px agent face. Only when you focus it does the app row fly out (translateY(-14px) scale(.86)) and the field stretch right.

Search

the bar today — search · apps · separator · agent
the face already carries presence: working spins, needs-you pulses, error goes red, and a badge counts notifications

the collision this creates

That last item is the problem. The dock already has an agent face with presence states. Put a rail of faces on the right edge and the shell has two homes for the same idea, both animated, both meaning “an agent wants something.” That has to be resolved on purpose, not left to whoever ships second.

Three resolutions, and the four designs below pick between them. Evacuate — the dock's face and separator are deleted; the household is the rail's job alone and the dock goes back to being a launcher. Delegate — the dock face stops being an identity and becomes a verb: ask, routed to whoever is on duty, while the rail is where you pick a person. Mirror — the dock shows whoever is currently pressed on the rail. I'd avoid mirroring: two places showing one state invites the question of which is authoritative, and there is no good answer.

question three

What is the rail made of?

The drawer's “underneath” is not an accident and not a shadow — it is five inset shadows and a 40ms delay, all painted by the panel itself because nothing can cast onto it from above. Here is the seam at 8× so the layers separate.

The slab's edgeThe cut face of the desktop, catching light. In light mode it darkens instead — dark mode has nowhere darker to go.inset 1px 0 0 --seam-edge
The groove behind itTwo pixels of nothing, so the edge has depth rather than being a drawn line.inset 2px 0 0 --seam-groove
The castThe desktop's slab throwing shadow down into the drawer. This is the layer doing most of the work.inset 12px 0 18px -10px --seam-cast
The lip, top and bottomShading at both ends so the drawer reads as sitting in a slot, not as a full-height column.inset 0 ±16px 20px -16px --drawer-lip
The 40ms lagContents are pinned at the open width and arrive a beat late, sliding 22px. This is the trick that sells “was already under there” over “grew out of the side.”animation: … 460ms var(--drawer-ease) 40ms

So the rail's material question is really: what is the rail, relative to that slot? Four answers, each of which becomes one of the designs below.

design 01Cut into the same slabThe rail is the slot's far wall — same sunk material as the drawer, same seam recipe, one continuous cabinet along the whole right edge. Caps are drawer fronts set into it.
design 02Raised over the seamNot part of the slot at all — a floating strip in ds-raised glass, the dock's own material. It belongs to the control layer, so it can hover above fullscreen apps.
design 03A shallow grooveNarrow and flush — a status channel more than a keyboard. Presence reads first, pressing second. Small caps mean many of them fit.
design 04A cabinet of drawersEach cap is the face of its own drawer. Pull one out and it stays out; pull a second and both are out side by side. The rail is the cabinet front.
01

push · cut · evacuate

The Cabinet

The pure form. Three slabs — frame, drawer, rail — with the rail cut from the same sunk material as the slot, so the entire right edge is one piece of furniture. The dock loses its agent face and goes back to being a launcher. One drawer out at a time.

dockevacuated — launcher only
materialcut into the slot's far wall
open at onceone

The move

A pressed cap squares off its left corners and slides over the seam, so the cap and the open drawer are visibly one object — the cap is the drawer's face. The order of the animation carries the physics: press (80ms), pull (460ms), fill (+40ms). Press first, then travel, then contents.

Why evacuate the dock

Because the dock's face was the door when there was one agent behind it. With five, a single face on the dock has to either lie about who it is or become a menu — and a menu of people, 40px from a rail of people, is the collision made permanent. Deleting it also gives the bar back ~60px.

Where it's weak

  • Costs ~64px of edge permanently, on every screen, forever.
  • Nothing above eight agents. The rail has no answer for scrolling that keeps it feeling like a set of people.
  • Losing the dock face means losing the one place a passing user learned there was an agent.

Cost

Small. A rail component, a third flex child, a per-agent session key, and a deletion in the dock. The drawer, the travel, the seam and the resizer are all reused unchanged.

02

overlay · raised · delegate

The Ledge

The opposite bet. The menubar spans the machine and never moves, the drawer floats over the deck, and the rail is a raised glass strip in the dock's own material — floating clear of the edge, with a gap top and bottom. It joins the control layer instead of the furniture, which buys it one thing nothing else here has: it survives fullscreen.

menubarspans, never moves
dockdelegated — the face means “ask”
materialds-raised glass, dock family
open at onceone

The one thing it buys

Reachability. Because it is the control layer and not the furniture, the rail can float above a fullscreen app the way the dock cannot — so an agent that needs you is one press away in a state where every other design has hidden the household completely.

What it gives up

The seam. Nothing here is “beneath” anything — the drawer casts a drop shadow onto the deck, and the five inset layers get deleted. It also re-introduces the half-covered surface the omnibar work spent a version removing.

Where it's weak

  • Two floating glass objects, the dock and the rail, in an L at the bottom-right. They will fight for the corner.
  • The drawer hides the right third of every window. You cannot watch an agent work in an app while talking to it.
  • “Ask” as a dock verb is a nice idea that needs its own routing design.

Cost

Small to medium. Cheapest layout (nothing reflows) but it throws away material that already exists and works, which is a real cost even though it doesn't look like one on a ticket.

03

push · groove · split menubar

The Mantel

Splits the difference on question one. The menubar's left half — the app caption — travels with the desktop, because it describes the desktop. Its right half — sync, clock, you — stays pinned at the machine's edge and becomes the head of the rail. The household reads as status hardware, so the caps get small and many of them fit.

menubarsplit — caption travels, status pinned
dockevacuated — launcher only
materialshallow groove, caps flush
open at onceone

The argument for splitting

The menubar is genuinely two things wearing one bar. frn-menubar-title is the desktop's caption; BackendStatus, the clock and the user button are the machine's. Splitting them stops the awkward question of whether “the menubar” travels — the two halves answer differently because they belong to different things.

What the narrow rail is for

Ten agents. At 31px the caps stop being keys and become status lights you can also press — which is the honest reading once there are enough of them that you are monitoring rather than conversing. Hover widens the rail and reveals names.

Where it's weak

  • The split is clever, and clever is a risk. A menubar that breaks in the middle when a drawer opens may just read as broken.
  • Small caps lose the keycap feeling — the physicality that made the direction worth choosing.
  • Two menubar segments means two backgrounds to keep aligned across themes and materials.

Cost

Medium. The Menubar component has to become two mounts with shared state, and the pinned segment has to survive fullscreen with the rail.

04

push · cabinet · two out at once

The Bank of Drawers

One change to design 01, and it is the one that makes the household feel like a household: more than one drawer can be out. Press a second cap and a second drawer pulls beside the first. Watch Stark work while Fern answers you. Two pressed caps, two open drawers, one desktop getting narrower — which is exactly the honest cost.

menubartravels with the desktop
dockevacuated — launcher only
materialcabinet — each cap is its own drawer
open at oncetwo (three on a wide screen)

Why this is the interesting one

Every other design here answers “who am I talking to.” This one answers “they work together” without inventing a group-thread concept: two agents are co-present because their drawers are both open, and you speak to whichever composer you click. Delegation becomes something you watch happen in the next drawer over.

The rule that keeps it sane

The cabinet has a capacity, not a scrollbar. MIN_DESKTOP_WIDTH (320) already exists — it becomes the constraint: pull a drawer that doesn't fit and the least-recently-used one pushes back in, physically. Nothing ever shrinks below a readable width.

Where it's weak

  • Two drawers at 23% each leave a narrow desktop. On a laptop this is close to unusable with any window open.
  • Focus gets ambiguous — two composers on screen, and Return has to go somewhere.
  • It is the most state to hold: which are open, in what order, at what widths, restored across reloads.

Cost

Medium. Design 01 plus a list of open drawers instead of one boolean, a width allocator, and a focused-drawer concept. Everything else is reused.

if you want my read

Build 01, leave the door open to 04

These are not four alternatives. Three of them are the same shell with one variable changed, and the fourth trades away the material for reach.

build

01 · The Cabinet

It is design 04 with the open-drawer list capped at one. Build it that way on purpose — a list of length one — and the second drawer is a later flag, not a rewrite. Keep the seam, evacuate the dock, push the desktop.

the flag

04 · Two out at once

Worth shipping the moment you have two agents actually working in parallel, and worthless before that. The width allocator is the only real new machinery; MIN_DESKTOP_WIDTH is already the rule it enforces.

borrow, don't build

03 · The Mantel

The narrow-groove rail is the right answer at ten agents and the wrong one at four — it trades the keycap feeling for density you don't need yet. Take the hover-to-reveal-names idea; leave the split menubar alone until the rail is genuinely crowded.

only for fullscreen

02 · The Ledge

Not a whole design — a mode. Fullscreen is exactly where the cabinet cannot push and the household disappears, so let the rail become raised glass there and the drawer overlay. One rule, two behaviours, and the material argument stays won everywhere else.

Mockups are stylized — UI is drawn oversized to read at this scale. Layout structure, the seam recipe, the dock's contents and the width constants are taken from src/shell/DesktopShell.tsx, src/shell/Omnibar.tsx and src/app.css.
