justapps.run · the way out

Four ways home

An app on justapps.run owns its whole subdomain, and its whole screen. Installed to a home screen it owns even more than that: no address bar, no back button, no tab — nothing that leads anywhere. These are four ways to give the screen back without taking it from the app, drawn over the same notes editor so you can see what each one collides with. Every frame is live. The question they answer differently is what the way out costs the app — from 32 px of reserved chrome down to nothing at all.

The way out belongs to the host, not the app. It is drawn by the page the runner serves, outside the mount point, so no app has to add it and no app can remove it.

The mock app has furniture at both edges — a toolbar on top, a formatting bar below — because an app with clear edges cannot show you what a way out costs.

The phone is not a screenshot. It is the same markup in a 390 px container, laid out by container queries.

d3 Shelf

The only direction that says what app you are in and who is hosting it — which is worth something in an installed app, where the browser has taken the address bar away and nothing else on screen answers either question. It is real reserved space, so it can never overlap the app; scroll down and it retracts, scroll up or reach for the top and it comes back.

Where I would land

d2 Beacon, with d3 Shelf as the fallback if the store would rather be a place than a set of unrelated origins. Three things decide it, and none of them are visible in the frames above — they are all about what the platform has already taken.

  1. The bottom edge is already spoken for

    Installed to an iPhone home screen, the system draws its own home indicator across the bottom of the screen. d1's capsule lands directly above it: two home bars, one of which is a lie. The direction that imitates iOS most faithfully is the one iOS has left no room for. In a browser tab it is perfect; installed — the case that actually strands you — it is not.

  2. So is the left edge

    In a browser tab, swiping in from the left edge is the browser's own back gesture, on iOS Safari and on Android Chrome. d4 cannot have it there. Installed, the gesture is free — so d4 works exactly where it is hardest to teach, and fails exactly where a first-time visitor meets it.

  3. Only d3 answers “where am I?”

    Installed, there is no address bar, so nothing on the screen says this app came from justapps.run. Whether that is worth 32 px is a product question, not a layout one: it is the difference between a store and a set of bookmarks.

Which leaves the Beacon. It costs no layout, collides with no platform gesture, behaves identically in a tab and installed, and moves out of the way when it is in the way. It is the only one of the four that is obvious the moment the app opens and gone by the second paragraph.

It is also nearly free to build. The way out belongs to the host, so it is rendered by the runner beside the mount point, where no app can reach it — and the tap needs no client code at all, just a link off this origin.

// apps/runner/src/app.tsx — in the body, after <div id="app">
<a class="beacon" href={`https://${c.env.ROOT_DOMAIN}/`} aria-label="All apps">…</a>

One prerequisite either way. The app document renders no <meta name="viewport"> today, so a phone lays it out at 980 px and scales down. Every direction here assumes that tag — and d1 additionally assumes viewport-fit=cover plus env(safe-area-inset-bottom), or its bar sits under the system's.
